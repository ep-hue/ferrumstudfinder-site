How to find wires in a wall before you drill

No handheld tool can promise that a spot in a wall is free of wiring. What you can do is lower the risk in three steps: know where cables usually run, check the spot with a tool that suits the job, and drill in a way that forgives a mistake. This guide covers all three, including what a phone can and cannot tell you.

Where cables usually run

  • Straight up and down from outlets and switches. The strip of wall directly above and below an electrical box is the most likely place to hit a cable.
  • Sideways between outlets. Cables often cross from one box to the next, commonly at about outlet height or through the studs a little above it.
  • Above and around switches, toward the ceiling. A switch feeds a light, so its cable normally heads upward.
  • Near panels, kitchens and bathrooms. More circuits meet in these walls, along with plumbing.

These are habits of the trade, not guarantees. Older houses, renovations and previous owners all produce surprises.

What an iPhone can and cannot tell you

A phone has no voltage sensor. It has a magnetometer, and a cable only produces a magnetic field when current is moving through it. That gives a phone app one honest job here: confirming that a loaded cable is at a certain spot.

To use Wire mode in Ferrum:

  1. Take off any magnetic case and calibrate away from the wall.
  2. Switch on something power-hungry on the circuit you are checking, such as a hair dryer, a space heater or a kettle. A phone charger or an LED lamp draws far too little.
  3. Rest the phone still against the spot for about three seconds. The app looks for the steady 50 Hz or 60 Hz rhythm of mains current, with nothing to set.

Read the result one way only. A positive reading tells you a loaded cable is close. No reading does not tell you the wall is clear: the cable there may be idle, on a different circuit, or too deep. Never treat a quiet phone as permission to drill.

Drill so that a mistake is survivable

  • Avoid the vertical strip above and below boxes whenever the job allows it.
  • Switch off the circuit at the breaker when you must drill near a box, and confirm it is dead at the outlet with a tester.
  • Limit the depth. Drywall is commonly about half an inch thick. A depth stop or a piece of tape on the bit, set just past the drywall, keeps the bit out of the cavity while you check what is behind.
  • Use a stud when you can. A screw into the center of a stud is stronger and stays out of the open cavity. See how to find a stud without a stud finder.
  • Stop if anything feels wrong. Unexpected resistance, a metallic scrape or a tripped breaker are reasons to stop and look.

If you are cutting a large opening, working in a wall with a panel, or unsure about what you found, call a licensed electrician. That costs less than repairing a damaged circuit.

Quick answers

Can an iPhone detect live wires in a wall?

Only when current is flowing. An iPhone's magnetometer can pick up the 50 or 60 Hz magnetic field of a cable that is powering a heavy load. It cannot sense voltage, so an energized cable with nothing switched on is invisible to it.

Does no reading mean there is no wire?

No. A phone app or a tester can miss a cable that is idle, deep or on another circuit. A quiet reading is never proof that a wall is clear.

Where do electrical wires usually run in walls?

Most often straight up and down from outlets and switches, and sideways between outlets at roughly outlet height. Renovations and older houses often differ, so treat this as a guide, not a rule.
